# Primer 1 — The Relationship Manager Role and How the Bank Makes Money

**Purpose:** Explain what a Relationship Manager (RM) in ICICI Bank's Wholesale Banking Group actually does, what products the RM sells and manages, and how each product turns into revenue for the bank.
**Reader:** MBA (finance), works in mutual fund marketing, NISM V-A certified. No prior banking or credit experience assumed.
**Running example:** A fictional client, *Sharma Precision Components Ltd*, a mid-sized auto-components manufacturer with about ₹400 crore annual sales. It returns in Primer 2, where its financial statements are analysed.

---

## 1. The bank in one page

### 1.1 What a bank is
A bank takes money from people and businesses who have spare cash (depositors) and lends it to those who need cash (borrowers). It earns the difference, and it also earns fees for services such as payments, guarantees and currency conversion.

### 1.2 A small, illustrative bank balance sheet (₹ crore)

| Liabilities (where the bank's money comes from) | ₹ | Assets (where the money is put to work) | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equity capital and reserves | 100 | Cash balance with RBI (CRR, about 3% of deposits) | 26 |
| Current and savings deposits (CASA) | 350 | Government securities held for SLR (about 18% of deposits) | 153 |
| Term deposits | 500 | Other investments (corporate bonds, CPs, etc.) | 60 |
| Borrowings (bonds, etc.) | 50 | Loans | 740 |
| | | Other assets | 21 |
| **Total** | **1,000** | **Total** | **1,000** |

Points to note:

- **CASA** (current account, savings account) is the cheapest money. Current accounts pay no interest. Corporate current accounts are a large part of why wholesale banking matters for CASA.
- **CRR** (Cash Reserve Ratio): the share of deposits a bank must keep with RBI as cash. It earns nothing.
- **SLR** (Statutory Liquidity Ratio): the share of deposits a bank must hold in government securities and similar safe assets. It earns a modest yield.
- Only what remains after CRR and SLR can be lent. This is why deposits are the raw material for lending.

### 1.3 Rough income statement of this bank (₹ crore, one year)

- Interest on loans: 740 × 9.0% ≈ 66.6
- Interest on investments: 213 × 6.8% ≈ 14.5
- **Interest earned ≈ 81.1**
- Interest paid: deposits 850 × 5.3% ≈ 45.1, plus borrowings 50 × 7% = 3.5, so about 48.6
- **Net Interest Income (NII) ≈ 32.5**
- **Net Interest Margin (NIM)** = NII ÷ earning assets (953) ≈ 3.4%

On top of NII, the bank earns **fee income** (guarantees, LC commission, processing fees, forex margins, cash management) and **treasury income** (trading gains on bonds). It then subtracts operating costs and credit losses to reach profit. Wholesale RMs influence all of these lines.

## 2. Wholesale banking: who the customers are and what they need

### 2.1 The JD's list of customers
The Wholesale Banking Group serves:

- Large private-sector business houses and companies
- Financial institutions and banks
- Public sector undertakings (PSUs) and Central/State government entities
- Multinational corporations (MNCs)
- Real estate companies
- IT and IT-enabled services (IT/ITES) and new-age services companies
- Financial sponsors: private equity funds and the companies they invest in
- Capital market and custody participants (served through digital solutions)

### 2.2 A company's money life, and the banking product for each need
Think of any company as a cycle. Each stage creates a banking need.

| Company's need | Banking product | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Build a factory or buy machines | Term loan / project finance | Sharma wants ₹50 crore to add a new machining line |
| Fund day-to-day operations (raw material, stock, credit to customers) | Working capital: cash credit, overdraft, working capital demand loan, bill discounting | Sharma buys steel today and is paid by its customers 80 days later |
| Buy from abroad | Letter of credit (LC), buyers' credit, forex | Sharma imports a machine from Germany |
| Give comfort to a counterparty | Bank guarantee (BG) | A customer asks Sharma for a performance guarantee |
| Collect money from many customers and pay many suppliers | Cash management / transaction banking | Sharma collects from 200 dealers every day |
| Protect against currency or rate moves | Forward contracts, swaps, options | Sharma owes $1 million in three months |
| Raise larger sums | Bonds, commercial paper (CP), syndicated loans, external commercial borrowing (ECB), IPO | A larger client raises ₹500 crore |
| Park surplus cash | Deposits, liquid funds, CDs, T-bills (Primer 4) | A cash-rich IT company |
| Pay employees | Salary accounts | Sharma's 600 workers |
| Promoters' personal wealth | Private banking, insurance, investment products | The Sharma family |

This table is the foundation of the **"360-degree banking"** idea in the JD: the RM looks at the whole list, not only the loan.

## 3. The RM's job in detail

### 3.1 The JD in plain words

| JD phrase | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| "Credit appraisal, financial analysis" | Study the company's business, industry, management and financials to judge whether it can repay |
| "Writing of credit notes" | Write the formal proposal document (see 3.3) that goes to the credit/risk approvers |
| "Client servicing" | Be the single point of contact: queries, limit changes, renewals, problem-solving |
| "End-to-end execution of banking transactions" | Make sure the sanctioned facility is actually set up, drawn, and works, with operations and product teams |
| "Documentation" | Loan agreements, security documents, board resolutions, registration of charges |
| "Covenant management" | Track the promises the borrower made and act if they are broken (see 5.4) |
| "Work under a Senior RM / Regional Head" | Junior role: you prepare and support; seniors negotiate and approve at higher levels |
| "360° banking, ecosystem" | Find more business from the client's suppliers, dealers, employees and promoters |
| "Fair to Customer, Fair to Bank" | Offer only what suits the client, at a price that is also fair to the bank; no mis-selling (see section 8) |
| "Role and location fungibility" | The bank can move you between roles and cities; expect a question on willingness to relocate |

### 3.2 Where new business comes from
The JD says the RM engages with banks, brokers, corporates, law firms, trade associations and chambers of commerce, and attends events and trade fairs. Why each matters:

- **Brokers / arrangers** bring borrowers who are shopping for loans.
- **Law firms and chartered accountants** advise companies on fundraising and introduce banks.
- **Other banks** share deals in consortiums (several banks lending to one borrower together).
- **Trade associations and fairs** give access to whole industries (e.g., an auto-components association to many vendors of one carmaker).
- **Existing customers' ecosystem**: the anchor's suppliers and dealers become new clients (see supply chain finance, 4.3).

### 3.3 What a credit note contains
A credit note (also called a credit appraisal memo) is the main document an RM writes. Typical parts:

1. Summary of the proposal: the limits requested, purpose, tenor, price, security
2. Borrower background and group structure
3. Management and promoters: track record, integrity, succession
4. Industry outlook and the borrower's position in it
5. Financial analysis: three years of actuals plus projections (Primer 2)
6. Facilities requested and how the company will repay
7. Security, guarantees and covenants proposed
8. External and internal credit ratings
9. Key risks and how each is reduced
10. Profitability of the relationship for the bank (see section 6)
11. Recommendation

The Risk team reviews it independently (the JD says "in collaboration with the Risk Management team"). The RM proposes; Risk challenges; a sanctioning authority or committee decides.

### 3.4 A typical week (illustration)

- Monday: review early-warning alerts on the RM's 25 accounts; chase a client's overdue stock statement
- Tuesday: meet a client CFO who wants to acquire a smaller rival; discuss how it could be funded
- Wednesday: sit with Risk to defend the credit note for Sharma's ₹100 crore proposal
- Thursday: work with operations on an LC amendment and a forward contract for another client
- Friday: attend a trade association seminar and meet a CA firm that refers clients

## 4. The product shelf, and how each product earns

### 4.1 Funded facilities (the bank actually lends cash)
**Term loan.** A lump sum repaid over several years, used for machinery, expansion or acquisitions. Repayment schedule usually has a moratorium period (no principal for some months) then instalments. The bank earns interest plus an upfront processing fee.

**Project finance.** Term loans for a specific large project such as a power plant or real estate development. The repayment comes from the project's own future cash flow, so the appraisal focuses on project cost, funding mix (debt vs equity), construction risk, and projected cash flows. Typical safeguards: an escrow account through which all project receipts pass, and a debt service reserve account (DSRA) holding a few months of instalments.

**Working capital.** Short-term money for operating needs. The main forms:

- **Cash credit (CC) / overdraft (OD):** a limit, say ₹60 crore, against which the company can draw and repay any day, paying interest only on the amount used. The maximum drawn is controlled by **drawing power** (Primer 2): typically a percentage of stock and receivables.
- **Working Capital Demand Loan (WCDL):** a fixed sum for a fixed short period within the working capital limit.
- **Bill / invoice discounting:** the company hands over an invoice due in 60 days; the bank pays about 97–98% today and collects the full amount from the buyer later. The bank's earning is the discount.
- **Packing credit (for exporters):** pre-shipment finance to produce goods for an export order, often in foreign currency.
- **Channel / supply chain finance:** see 4.3.

**Bank investment in a borrower's paper.** Banks also buy the client's bonds, NCDs or commercial paper. Economically this is lending, but it is tradeable (Primer 4).

### 4.2 Non-funded facilities (no cash goes out unless something goes wrong)
**Letter of Credit (LC).** The bank promises to pay a foreign seller once shipping documents are presented.
*Example:* Sharma wants to import a machine worth €500,000 from a German seller who does not know Sharma. Sharma's bank issues an LC: "If you ship the machine and present these documents, we will pay you." The seller ships with confidence. The bank charges a commission (for example 0.5% to 1% a year on the LC value) and, if the LC is usance (payable after, say, 90 days), it effectively also finances the buyer for that period.

**Bank Guarantee (BG).** The bank promises to pay a third party if the client fails to meet an obligation.

- *Performance guarantee:* "If Sharma fails to supply, the bank pays the customer a stated amount."
- *Financial guarantee:* backing a payment obligation.
- *Bid guarantee:* submitted with a tender.
The bank earns guarantee commission but is liable if the client defaults, so each one consumes the client's credit limit and some of the bank's capital.

**Why non-funded still matters for risk.** No cash leaves, but the exposure is real. For capital calculations the bank converts these into a loan equivalent using credit conversion factors: roughly 100% for financial guarantees (substitutes for credit), about 50% for performance-type guarantees, and about 20% for short-term trade LCs.

### 4.3 Transaction banking / cash management (the "plumbing")

- **Current accounts:** the client's main operating account. Large balances at zero interest are valuable CASA for the bank.
- **Collections:** for example, giving each of Sharma's 200 dealers a unique virtual account number so every payment is auto-identified and reconciled.
- **Payments:** bulk vendor payments, salary, taxes, electronically.
- **Escrow:** accounts that control the flow of money in real estate or project deals.
- **Supply chain finance:** The bank finances a strong **anchor** company's network. *Vendor finance:* suppliers of a large carmaker get paid early by the bank at a low rate because the bank relies on the carmaker's credit. *Dealer finance:* dealers get stock funding backed by the manufacturer's relationship. TReDS (an RBI-regulated invoice auction platform) is a related channel for small suppliers.
- **How the bank earns:** fees, and above all float (balances sitting in the bank). A client may keep ₹8–20 crore on average in a current account. To the bank this is funding at roughly zero interest cost.
- **Stickiness:** once a company's collections and payments run through one bank, switching is a large job. RMs value this because it keeps the relationship.

### 4.4 Forex and risk management products

- **Forward contract:** fix today the rate at which you will buy or sell currency on a future date.
*Example:* Sharma must pay $1 million in three months. Today the dollar is about ₹95.9. Sharma worries it will be ₹98 by then. The bank quotes a forward rate of, say, ₹96.7. Sharma locks ₹9.67 crore for certain. Whatever the market does, Sharma pays that.
The forward rate is usually a little higher than spot because Indian interest rates are higher than US interest rates. The difference is the **forward premium**.

- **Swaps** (currency or interest rate): exchange streams of payments, for example converting a floating-rate loan into a fixed-rate one.
- **Options:** give the right, not the obligation, to transact at a set rate. The buyer pays a premium.
- **How the bank earns:** a margin between the rate it quotes the client and the rate at which it covers itself in the interbank market. Each such contract uses up part of the client's credit line, because if the client cannot pay, the bank bears the loss.
- **Why this is sensitive:** selling complex derivatives to clients who do not understand them is a classic mis-selling risk. This is where "fair to customer" is tested.

### 4.5 Capital markets and advisory

- **Debt syndication and arranging:** the bank helps a company raise large loans or bonds from many lenders and earns a fee.
- **Underwriting / placement of bonds, NCDs, CP:** covered in Primer 4.
- **ECB (External Commercial Borrowing):** an Indian company borrows from foreign lenders, subject to RBI rules. A weak rupee raises the repayment burden unless hedged.
- **Equity capital markets, M&A advisory, structured finance:** usually involve the bank's investment banking group or group companies; the RM brings the client and coordinates.
- **Financial sponsors (PE funds):** they need acquisition financing, their investee companies need working capital and cash management, and their portfolio companies are additional customers.

### 4.6 Liability and wealth products for the ecosystem

- **Salary accounts for employees:** a company with 5,000 staff is 5,000 potential retail customers.
- **Promoter services:** family wealth, insurance and investment products via group entities.
This is the "One Bank, One Team" principle in the JD.

## 5. The credit lifecycle, step by step

### 5.1 Stages

1. **Identify and approach** the prospect (market scoping, referrals).
2. **KYC and basic checks:** identity, ownership, group, past credit record.
3. **Preliminary discussion / term sheet:** purpose, amount, tenor, rough price.
4. **Appraisal:** financial analysis, industry, management, site visits, peer comparison, ratings.
5. **Credit note** written and reviewed by Risk.
6. **Sanction** by the authorised committee, with conditions.
7. **Sanction letter** to the client; acceptance.
8. **Documentation and conditions precedent (CPs):** agreements, security creation, registration of charge with the Registrar of Companies and on CERSAI, insurance, board resolutions.
9. **Disbursement** only after CPs are met.
10. **Monitoring:** quarterly financials, monthly stock and debtor statements, account conduct, covenant checks, site visits.
11. **Review / renewal** annually or as the sanction requires.
12. **If things go wrong:** early warning, restructuring or recovery.

### 5.2 The "5 Cs" as a thinking aid
Character (integrity, track record), Capacity (ability to repay from cash flow), Capital (promoter's own money at stake), Collateral (security), Conditions (industry and economy). Capacity is the most important. Collateral is the last resort, not the main reason to lend.

### 5.3 Security, in simple terms

- **Primary security:** assets the loan finances, e.g., stock and receivables (charged by *hypothecation*), machinery bought with the term loan.
- **Collateral security:** extra assets, e.g., mortgage of land or buildings.
- **Guarantees:** corporate guarantee from a parent or promoter's personal guarantee.
- **Pledge of shares:** promoter shares handed over as security.
- **Escrow / Trust and Retention Account:** all receipts come through the bank's account.

### 5.4 Covenants
Covenants are the promises in the loan agreement.

- **Financial covenants:** e.g., DSCR must stay above 1.25; Total Outside Liabilities / Tangible Net Worth not above 3; current ratio at least 1.2.
- **Affirmative:** the borrower must do things, e.g., submit audited accounts in 180 days, maintain insurance, route sales receipts through the bank.
- **Negative:** the borrower must not do things without the bank's consent, e.g., raise more debt, pay large dividends, change ownership, give guarantees, sell key assets.
- **Information covenants:** quarterly results, stock statements, notice of any litigation.
- **Events of default:** missed payment, breach of covenant, false information, insolvency, cross-default (default on another lender's loan triggers default here).
*When a covenant is breached:* the RM finds out why, discusses with Risk, and the options include a waiver (perhaps for a fee), a reset of the covenant, a cure period, extra security, higher pricing, or in serious cases recalling the loan.

### 5.5 Stress and bad loans

- **SMA classification** (Special Mention Account) for early stress based on days overdue: SMA-0 (1–30 days), SMA-1 (31–60), SMA-2 (61–90).
- **NPA (Non-Performing Asset):** generally when interest or principal is unpaid for more than 90 days.
- **Provisions:** the bank must set aside money from profit against bad loans.
- **Recovery tools:** restructuring, SARFAESI Act (take possession of secured assets), Debt Recovery Tribunals, Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) through the NCLT.
Early warning signals the RM watches: delays in statements, sudden drop in account turnover, cheque bounces, rising stock or receivables, auditor resignation, tax or statutory dues unpaid, promoter share pledging, negative news.

## 6. How the bank makes money: one client, worked through

### 6.1 The client's request
Sharma Precision (sales ₹400 crore) asks us for a ₹60 crore cash credit limit and a ₹40 crore LC limit. It will also move its main current account and its collections to us, and expects about $10 million of forex transactions a year. (All figures are illustrative. Note: at ICICI, borrowers with turnover up to ₹750 crore are classed as Business Banking, so a real wholesale client would be larger than this example. ICICI's reported cost of funds is also lower than the 5.5% assumed here; see Primer 6.)

### 6.2 Yearly revenue to the bank (₹ crore)

| Source | Assumption | Revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Interest spread on cash credit | Average use 70% × ₹60 = ₹42 crore drawn; charge 9.25%; cost of funds 5.5%; spread 3.75% | 1.58 |
| Float on the current account | Average balance ₹8 crore; funding value about 5.5% | 0.44 |
| LC commission | Average LC outstanding ₹40 crore × 0.80% | 0.32 |
| Processing fee | 0.40% on ₹100 crore sanctioned (upfront, in year 1) | 0.40 |
| Forex margin | $10 million (about ₹96 crore) × 0.10% | 0.10 |
| **Total revenue** | | **2.84** |

### 6.3 Costs and capital

| Item | Assumption | ₹ crore |
|---|---|---|
| Operating cost | Staff, systems, branch allocation | 0.60 |
| Expected credit loss | Probability of default 1% × loss given default 45% × exposure ₹50 crore (₹42 crore drawn + LC at 20% conversion = ₹8 crore) | 0.23 |
| **Profit before tax** | 2.84 − 0.60 − 0.23 | **2.01** |
| Tax at about 25% | | 0.50 |
| **Profit after tax** | | **1.51** |

Capital held against this exposure: risk-weighted assets of about ₹50 crore (unrated corporate, 100% risk weight) × about 12% capital ratio = about ₹6 crore. Profit after tax of 1.51 on capital of 6 gives roughly 25% return on the capital used.

### 6.4 What this shows

- The loan spread alone (1.58) is only half of the relationship revenue. Fees, float and forex add the rest. This is why banks push cross-sell and why the JD asks for "360-degree" thinking.
- A large AAA-rated borrower pays a much lower spread, but also needs very little capital (risk weight of 20%) and loss provision, so the bank may accept it.
- A weaker borrower pays a higher price, but the expected loss and capital charge rise.
- Banks judge a relationship with **risk-adjusted return**, often called RAROC: risk-adjusted profit ÷ capital used.

### 6.5 Regulatory capital in short

- Banks must hold capital as a percentage of **risk-weighted assets (RWA)**. Minimum total capital ratio in India is 9%, plus a 2.5% conservation buffer, so 11.5%; larger systemically important banks hold more.
- Risk weights for corporates depend on external rating: roughly AAA 20%, AA 30%, A 50%, BBB 100%, below BBB 150%, unrated 100%.
- This is why an RM cares about a client's rating: the rating affects what the loan costs the bank in capital.

## 7. How loans are priced

- **Floating-rate loans** are linked to a benchmark. After RBI's reforms, many new floating loans to retail and small businesses are tied to an *external benchmark* such as the repo rate. Loans to large corporates may use repo-linked rates, MCLR (the bank's own marginal cost of funds), T-bill or other market benchmarks, depending on the product.
- **Price = benchmark + spread.** The spread reflects the borrower's credit rating, tenor, security, the relationship and competition.
- When the RBI changes the repo rate, loans linked to it reprice quickly. This is why the RBI's October 2026 decision (Primer 3) matters for clients.
- **Fixed-rate** long-term loans and bonds are priced off the government bond yield curve plus a credit spread (Primer 4).

## 8. "Fair to Customer, Fair to Bank"
What this means day to day:

- Offer what the customer actually needs (right product, right size, right tenor). Do not push a derivative on a client who cannot follow the risk.
- Disclose costs, risks and conditions clearly.
- Do not over-lend just to meet a target. A loan that is approved for the wrong reason harms both the customer and the bank.
- Protect the bank: sound documentation, correct security, and honest reporting of problems early.
- Follow KYC, anti-money-laundering, and conflict-of-interest rules.

A good interview answer here gives a real example of a time she had to balance a sales target against the client's interest (Primer 5).

## 1. The story in one page

1. **Until late 2025:** India was a fast-growing economy with low inflation. The RBI cut interest rates. US tariffs on Indian goods and foreign selling of Indian shares weighed on the rupee.
2. **28 February 2026:** The US and Israel attacked Iran. Iran closed the **Strait of Hormuz**, the waterway through which roughly one-fifth of the world's oil passes. Oil jumped above $100 a barrel within days.
3. **March to June 2026:** India imports most of its crude oil and gas, so the shock hit its import bill, inflation outlook and currency. The rupee fell to record lows near ₹96–97 per dollar in May. Foreign investors sold Indian equities. The government and RBI responded: higher gold import duty, curbs on silver imports, and a special scheme to attract dollar deposits from NRIs (the FCNR(B) swap window).
4. **July to August 2026:** The situation looked better. Foreign buying returned, India's GDP growth for April–June surprised on the upside at 7.8%, and reserves reached record levels.
5. **September 2026:** The improvement faded. The US Federal Reserve raised interest rates for the first time since 2023, US long-term bond yields rose to around 5% and above (highest since 2007), oil rose again after talks on reopening Hormuz stalled, Indian inflation ticked up, foreign investors turned sellers again, Indian bond yields rose to a two-year high near 7.2%, and stocks fell. Markets now expect the RBI to raise rates at its meeting on 5–7 October.

**The central idea:** India's economy is growing strongly at home, but it is exposed from the outside through oil, the dollar, and foreign money flows.

## 3. The growth picture

### 3.1 What GDP means and how to read it

- **GDP** = the value of goods and services produced in the country in a period. **Real** GDP removes price changes; **nominal** GDP includes them.
- **GVA** (gross value added) is GDP minus net taxes on products.
- Expenditure side: consumption (households, government), investment (GFCF), exports minus imports.

### 3.2 Q1 FY27 (April–June 2026) details

- Real GDP up 7.8% year on year; GVA up 8.2%.
- Manufacturing GVA up about 9.2%; services about 10%; the financial, real estate and professional services group grew about 12%.
- Investment demand (gross fixed capital formation) grew about 11.9%; private consumption about 7.1%.
- Bank credit to industry was growing faster than 19% and capital goods production was strong, which suggests a capex cycle. This is directly relevant to wholesale banking.
- Weak monsoon and the oil shock are the main risks to the rest of the year. The RBI's full-year forecast (6.7%) is well below the Q1 print.

### 3.3 Why it matters to an RM
Strong investment and industrial growth means more demand for term loans, working capital and trade finance. But high interest rates and a weak rupee can squeeze margins of customers who import inputs.

## 4. Inflation and the RBI

### 4.1 Basics

- India measures retail inflation with the **CPI** (new base year 2024 = 100). Food has a large weight.
- The RBI targets 4% inflation with a tolerance band of 2% to 6%.
- The RBI's main tool is the **repo rate**: the rate at which it lends to banks against government securities. It sits in a corridor: the **SDF rate** (5.00%, what banks earn by parking extra cash at the RBI) is the floor, and the **MSF rate** (5.50%, what banks pay for emergency borrowing) is the ceiling.
- The RBI also manages **liquidity** (the cash in the banking system) through operations such as VRRR auctions (banks lend their extra cash to the RBI for a set period, absorbing liquidity) and buying or selling bonds.

### 4.2 What happened

- Inflation was extremely low in late 2025, which let the RBI cut the repo rate by a total of **125 bps** from February 2025, ending at 5.25% in December 2025.
- After the war began, the RBI held rates at each meeting, describing itself as waiting for more clarity.
- Inflation has risen steadily: 4.45% in July and **4.82% in August 2026**. Economists at SBI and others have advocated a rate hike. Market pricing in mid-September implied roughly a 70% chance of a 25 bps hike on 7 October, and several economists expect a second hike in December.
- The RBI says it focuses on **headline** inflation rather than core.

### 4.3 Liquidity: a twist
Large inflows into banks through the FCNR(B) scheme (section 7) and the RBI's own dollar purchases pushed surplus rupee liquidity to a record level (around ₹10 trillion at one point). The RBI then absorbed some of it through VRRR auctions, but bids were weaker than the amounts offered. The RBI has also sold government bonds to soak up money. Surplus liquidity usually pushes short-term rates down; soaking it up pushes them back toward the repo rate.

### 4.4 Why it matters to an RM

- A repo hike raises the cost of floating-rate loans to clients and squeezes their interest cover (Primer 2).
- Banks' deposit costs rise with a lag; their loan yields on repo-linked books rise fast. The net effect on NIM depends on the mix.
- Bond portfolios lose value when yields rise (Primer 4).

## 5. The rupee

### 5.1 How an exchange rate is set
A floating currency is set by supply and demand for dollars. Demand for dollars comes from imports (especially oil and gold), foreign investors taking money out, and Indian companies repaying foreign loans. Supply comes from exports, services exports (IT), remittances, FDI and foreign investors bringing money in. The RBI buys or sells dollars to smooth sharp moves; it does not defend a fixed level.

### 5.2 What happened

- Late February 2026: around ₹90.9 per dollar.
- After the war started, oil, foreign selling and a stronger dollar pushed the rupee down. It touched about **₹96.8 in mid/late May**, a record low, about 5% weaker than before the war at that point.
- Since then it has been in a fairly tight range around ₹95.5–96.4; it was about ₹95.9 on 29 September.
- The RBI sold dollars to slow the fall. At the same time, the government and the RBI took further steps, described in sections 7 (FCNR) and 9 (gold and silver curbs).
- Rupee forecasts from global banks in May were for ₹96 by mid-2026 and ₹98 by end 2026.

### 5.3 Effects

| Who | Effect of a weaker rupee |
|---|---|
| Exporters (IT services, pharma, textiles, engineering) | Gain: same dollars earn more rupees |
| Importers (oil marketing companies, airlines, electronics, chemicals, fertiliser) | Lose: inputs cost more rupees |
| Companies with foreign-currency debt (ECBs) not hedged | Lose: repayment in rupees rises |
| Households | Higher prices for petrol, gold, electronics, foreign education and travel |
| The government | Higher import bill, inflation pressure |
| Foreign investors | Returns in dollars are lower, so they may sell |

### 5.4 Why it matters to an RM
A big part of the RM's conversation with clients in 2026 is **hedging**: importers with unhedged dollar payables are exposed, and exporters may want to lock in favourable rates. The bank earns forward-contract margins, but must sell responsibly (Primer 1).

## 6. Oil and the Strait of Hormuz

### 6.1 Why oil matters so much for India
India imports most of the crude oil it uses and most of its LPG, and a large share of both comes through or from the Gulf. Higher oil means:

1. A larger import bill, which widens the **current account deficit (CAD)** and puts downward pressure on the rupee.
2. Higher fuel, transport and fertiliser costs, which push up inflation.
3. Higher costs for companies (paints, chemicals, airlines, plastics), reducing margins.
4. Higher government subsidy or tax loss, affecting the **fiscal deficit**.

### 6.2 What has happened

- Before the war, Brent crude was roughly $60–65. In the first week of the war it crossed $100.
- It reached a peak of about $118 at the end of April (when US threats on the blockade intensified) and eased to a range of about $83–90 in August when a deal looked possible.
- In late September, Iran offered to reopen the strait within a week in exchange for a package (releasing frozen funds, easing sanctions, ending the US blockade of Iranian ports). The US President rejected the proposal, and Brent jumped to about $107 on 28 September. Saudi Arabia's Red Sea export terminal at Yanbu recovering from a pipeline attack helped prices ease to roughly $96–103 on 29 September.
- Shipping through the Strait has recovered to a level around 75% of pre-war, according to one tracking firm *(verify)*. The US Energy Information Administration expects Middle East production to return to near pre-war levels only in early 2027.

### 6.3 India's current position
The CAD for April–June was still small (0.5% of GDP) because services exports and remittances are large, but economists expect it to widen in the coming quarters. Reserves are high, which provides a buffer.

## 7. Foreign flows, reserves and FCNR

### 7.1 FPI and FDI: the terms

- **FII / FPI (Foreign Portfolio Investor):** a foreign fund buying Indian shares and bonds for returns. It can leave quickly ("hot money").
- **FDI (Foreign Direct Investment):** a foreign company setting up or buying a business long term. It is stable.
- Flows show up in the **balance of payments (BoP)**: current account + capital account = change in reserves.

### 7.2 What happened

- FPIs pulled ₹1.66 lakh crore from Indian equities in all of 2025. In 2026 they sold heavily between March and June, and net outflow reached about **₹2.45 lakh crore by 18 September 2026**, already far more than 2025.
- There was a break in July (about ₹20,000 crore of net buying) and August (about ₹30,000 crore); in September they sold about ₹21,000 crore until the 18th.
- The Q1 FY27 balance of payments data showed net FPI outflow of about $9.6 billion in April–June, and reserves fell by about $8.1 billion on a BoP basis in that quarter. They were then rebuilt by the FCNR(B) inflows.
- Domestic institutions (mutual funds, insurers) have been buying, which has cushioned the fall. **SIP money** is the steady source of this. This is something she will understand well from DSP.
- The reasons for selling: higher US yields and dollar (better returns at home for investors), high oil, a weak rupee, and a desire to reduce risk.
- There were also debt outflows: foreign investors sold Indian bonds too.
- FDI: one report says net FDI in recent months was at a five-year monthly high *(verify)*.

### 7.3 FCNR(B): what it is and why it mattered

- **FCNR(B)** = Foreign Currency Non-Resident (Bank) account. An NRI deposits dollars (or pounds, euros etc.) in an Indian bank for one to five years. The bank pays interest in the foreign currency; the NRI gets the money back in foreign currency, so the **NRI carries no rupee risk**. Interest is typically linked to global benchmark rates (SOFR).
- Because the bank now holds dollars it does not want to lend in rupees, it can **swap** them with the RBI. Under a **concessional swap window**, the RBI takes the dollars now and gives rupees, agreeing to return dollars at maturity at a fixed, favourable forward rate. The bank avoids currency risk and earns a cheaper source of funds; the RBI gets dollar reserves quickly.
- India used similar schemes in 2013 when the rupee was under attack.
- **2026:** The RBI opened a special swap window in June. By 21 August, banks had raised about **$65.4 billion** through FCNR(B) deposits. That is bigger than the $40–60 billion estimate at launch. The RBI then closed the window earlier than planned (deposits until 31 August; swaps until 11 September instead of 30 September and 16 October). The news pushed the 10-year bond yield up a few basis points because it raised concerns about future dollar inflows and liquidity.
- **Effect:** the inflows helped lift reserves to a record level and steadied the rupee, but also created surplus rupee liquidity.
- **Watch for:** the deposits mature in 1–5 years, so they must be rolled over or repaid in dollars later. Some analysts see this as buying time rather than fixing the cause.

### 7.4 Reserves
Total foreign exchange reserves reached a record of about $741 billion in the week to 28 August. At that level, reserves cover well over ten months of imports (about 11 months in one reporting). The RBI uses reserves to sell dollars and calm the rupee, and to back confidence.

## 8. Global bond yields and the Fed

### 8.1 Concepts (see Primer 4 for bond basics)
A **yield** is the return on a bond. When bond prices fall, yields rise. The **US 10-year Treasury yield** is the world's base interest rate: other rates are set as a spread above it. When it rises, money flows toward US assets and away from emerging markets like India.

### 8.2 What happened

- Early 2025 to 2026: US yields climbed as large government deficits, heavy borrowing, stickier inflation, AI-related investment and higher oil pushed them up.
- **16 September 2026:** the US Federal Reserve, led by Chair Kevin Warsh, raised the federal funds rate by 0.25 percentage points to **3.75–4.00%** in a unanimous 12–0 vote. It was the first increase since 2023. Officials' projections show more hikes possible this year.
- The US 10-year yield reached about 5%, its highest since 2007, and rose further to about 5.25% by 29 September. The 30-year is near 5.57%.
- This coincided with a sharp fall in gold and silver prices (section 9) and weaker emerging market markets.

### 8.3 Link to India

| Channel | Effect |
|---|---|
| Interest differential | Higher US yields reduce the extra return that Indian bonds offer, so foreign investors sell Indian bonds and equities |
| Dollar | Usually strengthens, which weakens the rupee |
| Indian bond yields | Rise in sympathy: the India 10-year yield rose from about 6.8% in mid-August to about 7.2% now |
| Equity valuations | Higher discount rates reduce what shares are worth |
| Corporates' foreign borrowing | ECBs become dearer; refinancing harder |

### 8.4 India's bond market in 2026

- The India 10-year government bond yield fell to lows near 6.5–6.6% in late 2025 after RBI rate cuts and bond purchases, rose as the war pushed oil up, and is now near a two-year high around 7.2%.
- Reasons: higher oil and inflation expectations, US yields, RBI selling bonds to absorb liquidity, fewer buyers among banks when liquidity is used up, and heavy government borrowing. The government reportedly trimmed its borrowing plan, but sentiment remains cautious.
- Inclusion of Indian bonds in global indices (JP Morgan from 2024, then Bloomberg and FTSE Russell) had brought in foreign buying in earlier periods; in the current environment the flows have reversed.

## 9. Gold and silver

- **Gold** is held as a safe asset in times of fear, as a hedge against inflation, and by central banks. It is priced in dollars, so a weaker rupee raises its rupee price.
- Over the past year gold rose about 28% in rupee terms (about ₹1.17 lakh to about ₹1.5 lakh per 10 grams). Silver rose about 60%.
- **In the last month** both fell (gold about 3–6%, silver about 5%), driven by the Fed rate hike, rising real yields, and high oil keeping rate expectations up. Gold does not pay interest, so when bonds yield 5% it is less attractive.
- **Policy response:** India raised the import duty on gold and silver from 6% to 15% (about May 2026), the Prime Minister asked citizens to delay non-essential gold purchases for a year, and there were curbs on most silver imports. Gold imports had hit a record of about $72 billion in the previous year, which was a major factor in the trade deficit and the rupee.
- For the RM: gold loans and jewellers are a separate lending segment; gems and jewellery exporters are affected by duty changes. For markets: gold ETFs and gold funds, and the end of new **Sovereign Gold Bond** issuance are relevant product topics.

## 10. Stock markets and sectors

- Indian equities: the Nifty 50 was about 25,500 in late February 2026 and is near 22,700 now, about 14% below its 52-week high and about 8% down over a year.
- Sector performance over one year: Pharma and metals up strongly; **IT** down about 18% year on year and about 31% below its high; FMCG down about 18%; realty, auto and consumer durables under pressure. Midcaps and smallcaps have been falling more than large caps.
- India VIX (a fear gauge) was about 13, not extreme.
- Foreign investors sold; domestic institutions bought.
- Why IT has suffered: a weak outlook for US clients, AI disruption worries, visa cost changes *(verify)*, and a weaker earnings season. Note that a weaker rupee normally helps IT exporters, but the other factors outweighed it.

## 12. Connecting the dots (one cause-and-effect chain)

```
War and Hormuz closure
   -> oil price up
        -> India's import bill up -> current account deficit widens -> rupee weakens
        -> fuel/food inflation up -> RBI cannot cut, may hike -> bond yields up
   -> uncertainty -> foreign investors sell shares and bonds -> rupee weakens further
US deficits + inflation -> Fed hikes -> US yields up + dollar up
   -> foreign money leaves emerging markets -> rupee weakens, Indian yields up
RBI response
   -> sells dollars (drains rupee liquidity) -> raises dollar supply via FCNR(B) swap
   -> gold/silver import curbs to reduce dollar demand
   -> likely rate hike to protect the currency and anchor inflation expectations
Result for companies
   -> higher interest costs, higher input costs, hedging needs
   -> exporters benefit, importers and borrowers in dollars suffer
Result for banks
   -> deposit and loan rates rise; bond portfolios lose value; credit demand still strong
```

## 14. How to talk about this in an interview (a 60-second version)
"India's growth is strong at about 7.8% in the last quarter, led by investment and services, but the external side is under pressure. The Middle East war and closure of the Strait of Hormuz pushed oil above $100, which widened the import bill and weakened the rupee to a record of about ₹96–97 in May. The RBI held repo at 5.25% and used tools like the special FCNR(B) swap window, which raised over $65 billion in dollar deposits, to rebuild reserves. More recently, the US Fed raised rates and the 10-year US yield went above 5%, so foreign investors have sold about ₹2.4 lakh crore of Indian shares this year, and Indian 10-year yields are near 7.2%. Inflation is 4.8% and markets expect the RBI to start raising rates in October. For a wholesale banker, that means borrowers face higher funding costs and currency risk, so hedging and working capital discipline become central to the relationship."

Follow with: "I would keep an eye on how this affects the customer's margins, particularly importers and those with dollar debt."

# Primer 2 — Financial Understanding: Statements, Ratios and How a Banker Reads Them

**Purpose:** Revise the finance an RM uses in credit appraisal: the three financial statements, the common ratios, working capital analysis, project appraisal basics, and warning signs.
**Reader:** MBA finance background; this is revision, so explanations are short but every idea has a worked number.
**Running example:** *Sharma Precision Components Ltd* (introduced in Primer 1). All numbers are in ₹ crore and are invented for teaching.

---

## 1. The big picture: what each statement answers

| Statement | The question it answers | Time view |
|---|---|---|
| **Balance sheet** | What does the company own, what does it owe, and what is left for owners? | A snapshot on one date (e.g., 31 March 2026) |
| **Profit and loss (P&L)** | Did the business earn more than it spent during the year? | A period (the whole year) |
| **Cash flow statement** | Where did cash actually come from and go to? | A period |

A lender cares about all three, but the question behind everything is: **"Will this company generate enough cash to pay interest and repay principal on time, even if things go a bit wrong?"** Profit is an accounting opinion; cash is what repays the loan.

---

## 2. The balance sheet

### 2.1 The equation
**Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity (net worth).**
Everything the company owns is paid for either by borrowing or by owners' money.

### 2.2 Items, in the order a banker looks at them

**Assets**
- **Fixed assets** (land, buildings, plant and machinery), shown net of accumulated depreciation.
- **Investments** (long-term), including investments in group companies. A banker checks whether money has been moved out to related parties.
- **Inventory:** raw material, work in progress, finished goods.
- **Trade receivables (debtors):** money customers owe.
- **Cash and bank balances.**
- **Other current assets:** advances, prepaid expenses, tax receivables.

**Liabilities**
- **Long-term borrowings (term loans, debentures).** The part due within the next year is shown as "current maturities".
- **Short-term borrowings:** bank working capital.
- **Trade payables (creditors):** money owed to suppliers.
- **Other current liabilities:** statutory dues, advances from customers, provisions.

**Net worth (equity)**
- Share capital plus reserves and surplus. **Tangible Net Worth (TNW)** = net worth minus intangible assets (goodwill, brand value), because intangibles cannot be sold to repay a loan.

### 2.3 Sharma's balance sheet, 31 March 2026 (₹ crore)

| Assets | | Liabilities and equity | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net fixed assets | 190 | Equity (share capital 20 + reserves 130) | 150 |
| Inventory | 80 | Term loans, non-current | 85 |
| Trade receivables | 90 | Term loan, current maturities | 15 |
| Cash and bank | 20 | Working capital borrowings (bank) | 80 |
| Other current assets | 20 | Trade payables | 50 |
| | | Other current liabilities | 20 |
| **Total** | **400** | **Total** | **400** |

Derived groups:
- **Current assets** = 80 + 90 + 20 + 20 = **210**
- **Current liabilities** = 15 + 80 + 50 + 20 = **165**
- **Total debt** = 85 + 15 + 80 = **180**
- **Total outside liabilities** (everything owed to others) = 400 − 150 = **250**

## 3. The profit and loss statement

### 3.1 Layout and what each line means
| Line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Revenue from operations | Sales of goods and services |
| − Cost of materials and direct costs | What it cost to make what was sold |
| − Employee costs | Salaries and benefits |
| − Other operating expenses | Power, freight, rent, etc. |
| **= EBITDA** | Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: operating profit in cash-like terms |
| − Depreciation and amortisation | Accounting charge for using up assets |
| **= EBIT** | Earnings before interest and tax: operating profit |
| − Interest expense | Cost of borrowing |
| **= PBT** | Profit before tax |
| − Tax | |
| **= PAT** | Profit after tax, which belongs to shareholders |

### 3.2 Sharma's P&L for FY2026 (₹ crore)
| Line | Amount | Margin on revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 400.00 | |
| Materials and direct costs | (260.00) | |
| Employee costs | (40.00) | |
| Other operating expenses | (30.00) | |
| **EBITDA** | **70.00** | 17.5% |
| Depreciation | (20.00) | |
| **EBIT** | **50.00** | 12.5% |
| Interest | (15.00) | |
| **PBT** | **35.00** | 8.75% |
| Tax at 25% | (8.75) | |
| **PAT** | **26.25** | 6.56% |

### 3.3 Quality-of-earnings points a banker checks
- **Exceptional or one-off items** (sale of an asset, a one-time write-back) inflating profit.
- **Other income** (interest, dividends, asset sale gains) forming too large a share of PBT. Lenders prefer core operating profit.
- **Revenue recognition:** is revenue booked before goods are delivered or money is reasonably expected?
- **Capitalisation of costs:** is the company moving ordinary expenses (even interest) onto the balance sheet to look more profitable?
- **Related-party sales:** sales to group companies that may be circular.

## 4. The cash flow statement

### 4.1 Three sections
- **Cash flow from operations (CFO):** cash generated by the core business after working capital changes.
- **Cash flow from investing (CFI):** buying or selling fixed assets and investments. Usually negative for a growing company.
- **Cash flow from financing (CFF):** borrowing, repaying loans, issuing shares, paying interest (under Ind AS, interest paid is in financing unless the company chooses otherwise) and dividends.

### 4.2 Why profit and cash differ: a sample
Sharma's PAT is 26.25. But the year's sales grew, so:
- Receivables rose by 20 (customers took longer or sales grew)
- Inventory rose by 20
- Payables rose by 8

Operating cash flow (indirect method, simplified):
| Item | ₹ crore |
|---|---|
| PAT | 26.25 |
| + Depreciation (non-cash) | 20.00 |
| + Interest (shown in financing) | 15.00 |
| − Increase in receivables | (20.00) |
| − Increase in inventory | (20.00) |
| + Increase in payables | 8.00 |
| **Cash from operations (before interest)** | **29.25** |

Although the company reported profit of 26.25, operations generated only about 29 before paying 15 of interest. Growth swallowed cash. This is why working capital lending exists, and why a fast-growing profitable company can still run short of cash.

### 4.3 Free cash flow
**Free cash flow (FCF)** = cash from operations − capital expenditure. It is the cash left for lenders and owners. A company with positive FCF can repay debt without borrowing more.

## 5. How the three statements connect: one transaction
Sharma buys a machine for 30, funded by a term loan of 30.
- **Balance sheet on day 1:** fixed assets +30 and term loan +30.
- **P&L over the years:** depreciation (say 3 per year) and interest reduce profit.
- **Cash flow:** investing −30 (the purchase); financing +30 (the loan); later, financing shows repayments and interest.
- **Next year's balance sheet:** fixed assets fall by depreciation; loan falls by repayments; cash and reserves change by retained profit.

A credit analyst who can trace one transaction through all three statements can spot entries that do not fit.

## 6. Ratios: the working toolkit
For each ratio: formula, reading, and Sharma's number.

### 6.1 Liquidity: can it pay short-term bills?
| Ratio | Formula | Sharma | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ Current liabilities | 210 ÷ 165 = **1.27** | Banks typically want at least 1.2 to 1.33. Below 1 means short-term trouble. |
| Quick ratio | (Current assets − Inventory) ÷ Current liabilities | 130 ÷ 165 = **0.79** | Stricter test, excluding stock that may take time to sell. |

### 6.2 Leverage: how much is borrowed?
| Ratio | Formula | Sharma | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Debt-to-equity | Total debt ÷ Net worth | 180 ÷ 150 = **1.20** | Higher means more risk. Acceptable level depends on the industry. |
| TOL / TNW | Total outside liabilities ÷ Tangible net worth | 250 ÷ 150 = **1.67** | A common loan covenant; often limited to 3 or less. |
| Net debt / EBITDA | (Debt − cash) ÷ EBITDA | 160 ÷ 70 = **2.29x** | Years of operating profit needed to repay debt. Many lenders look for under 3x to 4x. |

### 6.3 Coverage: can it pay interest and instalments from earnings?
| Ratio | Formula | Sharma | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interest coverage (ICR) | EBIT ÷ Interest | 50 ÷ 15 = **3.33x** | How many times earnings cover interest. Below 1.5 is concerning. |
| EBITDA / interest | EBITDA ÷ Interest | 70 ÷ 15 = **4.67x** | |
| **DSCR** | (PAT + Depreciation + Interest) ÷ (Interest + scheduled principal repayment) | (26.25 + 20 + 15) ÷ (15 + 15) = 61.25 ÷ 30 = **2.04x** | The most important lending ratio for term loans. Banks usually look for at least 1.25x, often 1.5x. |

*Why add depreciation back?* It is an expense that does not use cash. *Why add interest back?* Because interest is in the denominator as something to be paid.

### 6.4 Profitability
| Ratio | Formula | Sharma |
|---|---|---|
| EBITDA margin | EBITDA ÷ Revenue | **17.5%** |
| PAT margin | PAT ÷ Revenue | **6.56%** |
| Return on equity (ROE) | PAT ÷ Net worth | 26.25 ÷ 150 = **17.5%** |
| Return on capital employed (ROCE) | EBIT ÷ (Net worth + Debt) | 50 ÷ 330 = **15.2%** |

A healthy check: ROCE above the interest rate the company pays on its debt (here, 15.2% against roughly 8 to 9%) means borrowing adds value.

### 6.5 Efficiency and the working capital cycle
| Ratio | Formula | Sharma |
|---|---|---|
| Debtor days | Receivables ÷ Revenue × 365 | 90 ÷ 400 × 365 = **82 days** |
| Inventory days | Inventory ÷ Cost of goods × 365 | 80 ÷ 260 × 365 = **112 days** |
| Creditor days | Payables ÷ Cost of goods × 365 | 50 ÷ 260 × 365 = **70 days** |
| **Cash conversion cycle** | Debtor days + Inventory days − Creditor days | 82 + 112 − 70 = **124 days** |
| Asset turnover | Revenue ÷ Total assets | 400 ÷ 400 = **1.0x** |

Reading the cycle: Sharma pays its suppliers after 70 days, but it takes 82 + 112 = 194 days to turn material into collected cash. So for about 124 days every rupee of sales is being funded by someone else's money, mostly the bank's. Lengthening cycles are the most common early sign of stress.

### 6.6 The DuPont breakdown of ROE
**ROE = PAT margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier** (total assets ÷ equity)
Sharma: 6.56% × 1.0 × (400 ÷ 150 = 2.67) = **17.5%**.
This shows whether ROE comes from good margins, efficient use of assets, or simply high borrowing. A company with high ROE mainly from leverage is riskier.

### 6.7 Growth
Revenue growth, EBITDA growth, PAT growth. Compare with the industry, and check if growth is paid for by stretching receivables or debt.

## 7. Working capital assessment

### 7.1 Idea
Working capital is what a business needs to fund its operating cycle.
**Working capital gap** = Current assets − Current liabilities other than bank borrowings.
Sharma: 210 − (50 + 20 + 15) = 210 − 85 = **125**.

### 7.2 Traditional methods (still used as reference, not compulsory rules)
- **Turnover method (small borrowers):** bank finances about 20% of projected turnover, with 5% from the promoter.
- **Maximum Permissible Bank Finance (MPBF), Tandon Committee methods.** The idea is that the promoter must fund part of working capital with long-term money, so the bank does not finance the whole gap.
 - *Method I:* the bank finances 75% of the working capital gap. For Sharma: 75% × 125 = **93.75**.
 - *Method II:* the promoter must fund 25% of total current assets, so MPBF = gap − 25% of current assets = 125 − (25% × 210 = 52.5) = **72.5**. This is the stricter method.
 Sharma's existing bank borrowing is 80, which sits between the two benchmarks. A banker would ask which basis the existing limits were set on, and whether the company's own long-term funding of working capital is adequate.
- Today banks apply their own assessment (cash budget, operating cycle), using these as benchmarks.

### 7.3 Drawing power
Because cash credit is a flexible limit, the amount Sharma can draw at any time is limited by **drawing power (DP)**:
DP = (Value of stock and receivables) − Margin (promoter's own contribution, e.g., 25%) minus excluded items (e.g., receivables older than 90 days).
If stock is 80 and eligible receivables 90, at 25% margin: DP = 0.75 × 170 = **127.5**, above the sanctioned 80, so the limit itself binds.
The bank reviews **monthly stock and debtor statements** to recompute DP. Inflated statements are a known fraud route.

### 7.4 Overtrading and window dressing
- **Overtrading:** growing sales faster than the company's funds can support, leading to reliance on short-term borrowing.
- **Window dressing:** temporarily improving ratios at year end, for example by delaying purchases or using a short-term loan to repay another. Checking monthly data rather than year-end numbers exposes it.

## 8. Term loan and project appraisal basics

### 8.1 Cost of project and means of finance
Suppose Sharma wants a new line costing **₹50 crore**. A typical financing mix:
- Term loan: 70% (₹35 crore)
- Promoter equity and internal accruals: 30% (₹15 crore)
The 70:30 (debt:equity) split is a common benchmark, but it varies by sector and risk.

### 8.2 Key checks
- **Projected cash flows and DSCR** year by year, in base and stressed cases (sales 10% lower, costs 5% higher, delay of 6 months).
- **Break-even point:** the sales level at which profit is zero.
- **Project risks:** technology, raw material supply, approvals, customers, construction delay and cost overrun.
- **Promoter contribution** brought in first (or pro rata) before the bank disburses.

### 8.3 Finance theory used here
- **Time value of money:** ₹100 today is worth more than ₹100 a year later, because it can be invested. **Present value** = Future value ÷ (1 + r)^n.
- **NPV** (net present value): PV of future cash flows minus the cost. A project with NPV > 0 adds value.
- **IRR:** the discount rate at which NPV = 0. If IRR exceeds the cost of capital, accept.
- **Payback period:** years to recover the investment; ignores time value and later cash.
- **WACC** (weighted average cost of capital) = weight of equity × cost of equity + weight of debt × cost of debt × (1 − tax rate). This is what a project must earn.
- **CAPM:** cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium. In India the 10-year government bond yield is commonly used as the risk-free rate. When it rises (Primer 3), the required return on every project rises.

*Quick example:* Sharma's new line costs 50 and adds 12 a year of after-tax cash flow for 7 years. At a 12% discount rate, PV of inflows = 12 × 4.564 = 54.8 (annuity factor for 7 years at 12% is about 4.564). NPV = 54.8 − 50 = **+4.8**, so it adds value. At a 14% rate (factor 4.288): PV = 51.5, NPV = +1.5, still positive but thin. A bank would want to see that the project survives a rate and demand shock.

## 9. Reading beyond the numbers

### 9.1 Sections of an annual report that matter
- **Auditor's report.** A qualified or adverse opinion, or "emphasis of matter" paragraphs, are serious. Note also the CARO statements about defaults, diversion of funds and related parties.
- **Notes to accounts.** Contingent liabilities (unbooked claims, guarantees given), related-party transactions, details of borrowings and security, ageing of receivables, litigation.
- **Directors' report / management discussion.** Strategy, risks, outlook.
- **Ratings reports** by CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, India Ratings, Acuité or Brickwork: they give an independent view and a **rating scale** (AAA highest, through AA, A, BBB, BB, B, C, D for default). Short-term ratings run from A1+ to A4 and D.

### 9.2 Red flags checklist
- Receivable or inventory days rising faster than sales
- Profit rising but operating cash flow falling or negative (for years)
- Repeated short-term borrowing used to fund long-term assets
- Loans and advances to group entities rising; sales to related parties
- Auditor changes or resignations, late filing of accounts
- Promoter shares heavily pledged
- Dues to tax authorities, PF or employees unpaid
- Large contingent liabilities compared with net worth
- Frequent restructuring or rollover of debt; credit rating downgrades
- Account conduct: constantly at the limit, cheque returns, irregular turnover

## 10. Reading a bank's own numbers
A wholesale RM is asked about the bank as well as the client.
| Metric | What it tells |
|---|---|
| NIM | Profitability of lending; higher is better |
| CASA ratio | Share of cheap deposits; higher means lower funding cost |
| Gross NPA / Net NPA | Share of loans that have turned bad before and after provisions |
| Provision Coverage Ratio (PCR) | Share of bad loans already provided for |
| Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR), CET1 | Loss-absorbing buffer relative to risk-weighted assets |
| Credit-to-deposit ratio | How much of deposits are lent |
| Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) | Whether the bank holds enough high-quality liquid assets (mostly government securities) for 30 days of stress |
| Return on assets (ROA) / Return on equity (ROE) | Overall profitability |
| Cost-to-income ratio | Efficiency |

*Note:* I have not researched ICICI Bank's current figures for these; look at its latest quarterly results and annual report before the interview.

## 11. Equity valuation basics (for cross-reference with Primer 4)
- **P/E** = Price ÷ Earnings per share. Earnings yield = 1 ÷ P/E. If the 10-year bond yields 7.2% and a stock has earnings yield of 4.5% (P/E of about 22), the stock has to grow to justify the lower current yield.
- **P/B** = Price ÷ Book value per share (used mostly for banks).
- **EV/EBITDA** = (Market cap + Net debt) ÷ EBITDA; removes the effect of capital structure.
- **Dividend yield** = Dividend per share ÷ Price.

## 12. Mini-exercise: read two years of Sharma

| Metric | FY2025 | FY2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 340 | 400 (+17.6%) |
| EBITDA (margin) | 62 (18.2%) | 70 (17.5%) |
| PAT | 22 | 26.25 |
| Total debt | 140 | 180 |
| Net debt / EBITDA | 1.97x | 2.29x |
| Debtor days | 75 | 82 |
| Cash conversion cycle | about 105 days | about 124 days |

**Reading it.** Sales grew strongly and profit grew with it, which is good. But margin slipped a little, debt rose faster than earnings, and the cash conversion cycle lengthened by almost 20 days, meaning growth is being funded by debt and by customers paying more slowly. The analyst's next questions: Which customers are paying late? Is inventory building up for a reason (new product, price rise expected)? Is the ₹100 crore request to fund growth, or to cover a cash squeeze? What do the cash flow statement and monthly account data say?
